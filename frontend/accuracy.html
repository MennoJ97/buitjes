How accurate is the forecast?

Every forecast frame the map shows is scored against the radar that later measured the same moment, over the whole map. Each one is set against persistence: the radar at the moment the forecast was made, held still. That is the forecast you get for free, so it is the one worth beating. What the terms mean is further down.

Is the rain in the right area?

Fractions skill score at 1 mm/h, in 25 km squares: did about the right share of each square get rain? 1 is perfect. Above the dashed 0.5 line the forecast is usually called useful at that scale. The time axis is stretched at the short end, where most of the change happens.

Rain on the exact square kilometre

Critical success index at 1 mm/h, pixel by pixel. Much stricter than the chart above: a shower one kilometre out of place counts as both a miss and a false alarm.

Too much rain, or too little?

Bias: how much map area the forecast painted at 1 mm/h or more, against how much the radar measured. 1 is the right amount, 1.2 is a fifth too much.

Is the uncertainty band honest?

The map's Low and High are the 10th and 90th percentile of the twenty ensemble runs. If they are honest, the radar lands above High about 10% of the time and below Low about 10% of the time. Counted only where either the band or the radar had rain nearby.

Day by day, one hour ahead

Top: the same score as the first chart, one hour ahead, for each day. Bottom: how much of the map had rain of 1 mm/h or more that day. A day with almost no rain scores erratically, because a handful of showers decides it. A missing point is a day with no rain to score.

By month

Compare months on skill against persistence rather than on the raw scores. Showers in summer are harder to forecast than rain bands in winter at every lead time, so the raw scores mostly say how hard the weather was. The gap over persistence says how good the forecast was.

What the terms mean

Lead time
How far ahead a forecast looks, counted from the radar picture it started from. "+1 h" is a forecast for one hour after that picture.
Persistence
The simplest possible forecast: assume the rain stays exactly where the radar has it now, at the same strength. It is very good for the next few minutes and gets worse quickly as showers move and change. A forecast earns its keep only where it beats persistence.
1 mm/h
The rain rate most scores here are counted at: rain you would notice and want an umbrella for. Below that is drizzle, which the radar and the forecast both find hard to place.
Critical success index (CSI)
Of all the pixels where the forecast or the radar (or both) had rain, the share where both did. 1 is perfect, 0 means no overlap at all. In the table, POD (probability of detection) is the share of measured rain the forecast caught, and FAR (false alarm ratio) is the share of forecast rain that did not happen.
Fractions skill score (FSS)
Instead of asking "did it rain on this pixel?", it asks "did about the right share of this square get rain?". A shower a few kilometres out of place then counts as nearly right rather than as a miss and a false alarm. Shown for 25 km squares; the table also has 5 km. Above 0.5 is the usual rule of thumb for "useful at that scale".
Bias
The area the forecast painted with rain, divided by the area that actually had rain. Above 1 means it spreads rain over too much of the map, below 1 too little. A forecast can have a perfect bias and still put every shower in the wrong place; that is what the other scores are for.
The band (Low and High)
The forecast is twenty runs, not one. The map's Low and High are the 10th and 90th percentile of what those runs put within a few kilometres of each point. An honest band is breached on each side about 10% of the time. More often above means real rain tends to be heavier than High suggests; more often below means it tends to be lighter than Low.
Skill against persistence
(FSS of the forecast − FSS of persistence) ÷ (1 − FSS of persistence). 0 means no better than holding the radar still, 1 means perfect, and below 0 means persistence did better.
Mean absolute error
The average difference in rain rate between forecast and radar, over every measured pixel, in mm/h. Small because most of the map is dry most of the time; useful for comparing lead times, not as a number on its own.
What is and is not counted
The whole map, as published, against KNMI's real-time radar. Pixels the radar could not see are left out on both sides rather than counted as dry. Cycles from the stand-in forecast, used while KNMI's main product is unavailable, are left out of every number here.
